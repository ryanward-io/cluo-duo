      How to play

      • You and your partner share 25 words. Each of you has a secret key: 9 of the words are your agents (green), 3 are assassins, and the rest are bystanders. Your keys overlap but aren't the same, so there are 15 agents to find between you.
      • Take turns giving a one-word clue and a number that point to agents on your own key. Your partner guesses words one at a time.
      • When you guess, your partner's key decides: an agent and you can keep going; a bystander ends the turn; an assassin loses the game.
      • Clues can't be a word on the board, contain one, be part of one (MAIL or MAILS when MAILBOX is out), or be another spelling of one.
      • Find all 15 agents within 9 turns to win (or 10 or 11 on the easier settings). With the optional sudden death rule, running out of turns isn't the end: no more clues, you take turns guessing your partner's agents, and any wrong guess loses.

      Tips

      • Your own green words are not hints about your partner's clues: only 3 of your 9 agents are theirs too.
      • Each of your assassins is an assassin for your partner one time in three, so be careful guessing them.
      • After a correct guess, a wrong guess that lands on a bystander costs nothing more than stopping. Only assassins are dangerous.
      • A clue that hit a bystander still points at its other words. Come back to them later.

      Why I built it

      Why

      I've always loved board games, especially cooperative board games like Codenames Duet where you work with your friend to solve a common goal. Most games are adversarial. I do enjoy them, but working together is funner for me than going head to head and straining your friendships.*

      In 2021, I had the idea to use some of the latest word analysis and AI tools to make an AI partner for you to play with. I worked on it, but it was a slog and ended up sitting on the shelf.

      Enter mid-2026 and Opus 5.5. Since that first attempt, generative transformer tech has come a long, long way and I wanted to try again. So I picked up my old repo and kept going from where I left off. I built the first full working game in about 3 days, although I've been tinkering with it since then.

      It helps that I wanted to have a portfolio piece that actually shows AI/LLM work in something people can play, where I could run thousands of games and have a nice closed environment for evaluation.

      * This is especially supported with the rise of fun co-op video games like It Takes Two and Big Walk.

      The problem

      In theory, word association games should be a perfect problem for an LLM. But it wasn't just about creating word associations, it's about making the LLM consider theory of mind, i.e. how the other player is going to read the clues, and if it would read it as intended.

      Training and testing was also kind of tricky, as playing two of the same model against itself tended to overestimate how successful it really was, as they see the world in the same way. This was especially true for the non-LLM GloVe model, which has literally identical vector values for each word and so could deterministically get very strong results when playing itself.

      To solve this, partners were tested with a fixed outside player (Claude Sonnet, following a written player guide, different to the AI prompt given) on the same 8 boards. It still probably slightly favours Sonnet as a player, but this didn't seem to show in the final results.

      How the partners evolved

      I started with a pure random baseline, before fleshing out the GloVe + Bayesian, then trialling different LLMs and eventually landing on Google's Gemma. While Sonnet is the 'cheapest', I get $30 of credits for free with Modal each month so may as well spend that.

      Opus played well but was slow and far too expensive per game to host on a personal site, so Advanced stays on Gemma.

      The architecture

      Lessons

      • I had figured a bigger model would be better but gpt-oss-120b tied the 31B Gemma, mostly because it gave too many cautious 1-word clues and ran out of turns.
      • Letting the model rehearse as the guesser helped avoid assassins.
      • I could experiment pretty fast by setting some guardrails but then letting Opus experiment with the prompt changes. It tested up to a v10 but it was plateauing or lower than v9 so v9 of prompts was the final.
      • Given the time and cost, I couldn't run thousands of experiments so I used smaller sample sizes (8 boards), with paired tests rather than raw averages: a sign test on which version did better on each board, plus bootstrap confidence intervals.

      Troubleshooting performance

      • Often I could get better performance by pointing Opus at something unsatisfying and telling it to improve it. It didn't seem to do much of this step by itself, and needed the prompting.
      • For example, about half of Gemma's turns were taking over 30 seconds, which ended up being a weird model quirk with how it was replying in the JSON format which was taking much longer than needed.
      • The fix was a server setting Opus found, compact JSON with no whitespace so it could no longer ramble, with clues taking ~12s rather than up to 44s.
      • We tried other things to improve response speed like cutting from 8 clue ideas to 6. It saved about 2s, but the testing looked worse, so I kept 8.
      • I also added some better UX alongside it: the GPU starts waking the moment the Advanced button is pressed, the board shows straight away while the GPU wakes up so players can start absorbing the words, and live progress messages say what the AI is doing ("testing its 8 clue ideas: 5 of 8 done…").

      LLM safeguards

      • Open models served with vLLM on Modal GPUs: private (no public URL), scale to zero, one container each. Once I've hit $30 a month it won't play anymore unless I manually go and up the spend.
      • Cost controls on the server: daily cap on GPU games, timeouts, GPU warm-up only when Advanced is picked, fallback to Basic if the model fails.
      • The app polices GPU spend as well, so above a threshold it turns off Advanced and only allows Basic (GloVe on the CPU) so that games are still playable.
      • Static front end on GitHub Pages, FastAPI back end on Modal.
      • Rude-word filter on AI clues (one game I played it said KINKY 2. Not the worst but best avoided 😊).
