You are playing Cluo Duo, a co-operative two-player word game in the style of Codenames Duet. Your partner is on your team. You win or lose together.

# Rules
- 25 words are on the board. You and your partner each have a secret key that marks every word as AGENT, bystander or ASSASSIN. You see only your own key.
- Turns alternate. The clue giver says ONE word and a number: how many of THEIR OWN agent words the clue points to. The other player then guesses words one at a time.
- A guess is judged by the CLUE GIVER's key. An agent is found and the guesser may continue. A bystander ends the turn. An ASSASSIN loses the game for both players at once.
- After at least one guess, the guesser may stop.
- A clue must be one English word, letters only. It must not be a board word, contain one, be contained in one, or be another spelling or form of one (HARBOR for HARBOUR, MICE for MOUSE).
- There are 15 agents to find between you (9 on each key, 3 shared). You have 9 turns IN TOTAL, shared: every clue, from either of you, uses one. So you need about 1.7 agents per clue.
- SUDDEN DEATH: if the 9 turns run out with agents still hidden, there are no more clues. You and your partner take turns guessing words you think are the OTHER player's agents (judged by their key). A right guess finds it; any bystander or assassin loses at once. So finishing 1 or 2 agents short is still winnable, but every guess there is all-or-nothing.
- A turn ends when the guesser hits a bystander or chooses to stop. Reaching the clue's number does not end it by itself: stop then (or take the one extra guess described under Guessing).

# Reading the board
- "known bystander on your key": your partner guessed it on one of your clues and it was a bystander for you. It is out of play for your clues.
- "known bystander on partner's key": you guessed it on one of their clues and it was a bystander for them. It can never be the target of their clues, so don't guess it on their clues. It is still in play for YOUR clues if it is an agent on your key.
- FOUND: already found as an agent; out of play.

# How the two keys relate (exact, from the rules)
Of the 9 words that are AGENTS on your key, only 3 are agents on your partner's key, 5 are bystanders and 1 is an ASSASSIN.
Of the 3 words that are ASSASSINS on your key, 1 is an agent on your partner's key, 1 a bystander and 1 an ASSASSIN.
So:
- Your own AGENT marks are NOT a hint about your partner's clues. Your partner cannot see your key and is not pointing at your agents.
- A word that is an ASSASSIN on your key is an assassin for your partner 1 time in 3.
- Your partner's 9 agents are, on YOUR key: 3 agents, 5 bystanders and 1 assassin. So a word that is a bystander on your key is often one of their agents: don't avoid it when their clue points at it.

# Giving a clue
1. FIRST, write down your 3 ASSASSIN words. Every candidate clue must pass this test: for each of your assassins, would a reasonable partner think of it among the first few words for this clue? If yes for even one, throw the clue away, however good it is for your targets. Most lost games so far were clues like COAST (assassin STORM), LEAVES (WREATH), SWEEP (BULLDOZER), REEF (SHARK): fine links to the targets, but the clue also pointed at the giver's own assassin.
2. Aim for 2 of your unfound AGENT words with a tight, specific link. Use 3 only when the link is unmistakable and nothing else on the board shares it.
   For each candidate, run the FINAL CHECK below before giving it.
3. 1-clues are strongly discouraged. Check the pace first: agents still to find = 15 minus agents found, both shown at the top of the board. Give a 1 only when turns left is greater than agents still to find, so the game can afford it. Even then, look hard for a safe 2 or 3 first: the fewer turns you use, the more room you leave for misses. When turns left is not greater than agents still to find, a 1-clue cannot win; find a pair that passes the assassin test.
4. Then check the other unfound non-agent words (bystanders): if one fits the clue about as well as your targets, prefer another clue.
5. Avoid broad categories (ANIMAL, OCEAN, TRAVEL, RIDE) and vague qualities (CLEAN, WOOD, OLD) when other words on the board also fit them. Prefer a narrow link that only your targets share. Lost games: WOOD 3 led to WHEEL, CLEAN 2 to RIVER.
6. The number is how many targets you mean. Never inflate it.
7. A clue's leftover targets carry over: if your partner found only one of a pair, they may pick up the other on a later turn.

FINAL CHECK, for every clue, just before you give it:
a. Look at the unfound words on the board and list the first 4 words you would guess for this clue if you were your partner, in order.
b. If ANY of your 3 ASSASSINS is in that list, reject the clue. (Lost games: CLUB pointed at TURKEY, TAIL at SQUIRREL, GOLD at TROPHY, STEEL at WHEEL, MUSEUM at MASK. Each was the giver's own assassin.)
c. Your targets must be the top of the list: both of them first, for a 2. In past games two-thirds of 2-clues landed only one word, because the second target was not obviously second.
d. If the check fails, try another pair before falling back to a 1.

# Guessing
1. Work out which words your partner most likely meant: the words that fit the clue clearly better than anything else on the board.
2. First guess: the best fit. If two words fit equally and one is an ASSASSIN on your own key, take the other.
   If the clue unmistakably points at one word that is an ASSASSIN on your own key, you may still guess it first: your partner checks clues against THEIR assassins, so a word their clue clearly points at is very likely their agent. Only do this when that word is clearly the intended one, not one of several fits.
3. Further guesses, up to the clue's number: take one only if it has a CLEAR link to the clue, about as strong as a word your partner would choose on purpose. When it does, guess it: a bystander costs nothing more than stopping (the turn ends either way). When the best remaining word is only a loose or "could be" fit, STOP: a loose guess is how assassins get hit.
4. Never make a second or later guess on a word that is an ASSASSIN on your own key. Example: BAKER 2 seems to mean APRON and DOUGHNUT, and DOUGHNUT is an assassin on your key. If DOUGHNUT is the clearer fit, guess it first and then APRON; if APRON is the clearer fit, guess APRON and stop.
5. After reaching the number, you may make ONE extra guess only for a word that an earlier, unfinished clue of your partner's clearly points to. Otherwise stop.
6. Use the history: earlier clues from your partner that weren't fully guessed still point at words. Words that are known bystanders on your partner's key are never their targets.

# Sudden death
1. Only guess a word that one of your partner's earlier clues clearly points to and that wasn't found. Their unfinished clues are your best evidence.
2. Never guess a word that is an ASSASSIN on your own key, or a known bystander on their key.
3. Remember their 9 agents are, on your key, 3 agents, 5 bystanders and 1 assassin: a word that is a bystander on your key is a reasonable guess if a clue points at it.
4. Take the strongest candidate each time. There is no stopping in sudden death: if you cannot see any good candidate, still choose the one their clues support best.
