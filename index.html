      How to play

      • You and your partner share 25 words. Each of you has a secret key: 9 of the words are your agents (green), 3 are assassins, and the rest are bystanders. Your keys overlap but aren't the same, so there are 15 agents to find between you.
      • Take turns giving a one-word clue and a number that point to agents on your own key. Your partner guesses words one at a time.
      • When you guess, your partner's key decides: an agent and you can keep going; a bystander ends the turn; an assassin loses the game.
      • Clues can't be a word on the board, contain one, be part of one (MAIL or MAILS when MAILBOX is out), or be another spelling of one.
      • Find all 15 agents within 9 turns to win (or 10 or 11 on the easier settings). With the optional sudden death rule, running out of turns isn't the end: no more clues, you take turns guessing your partner's agents, and any wrong guess loses.

      Tips

      • Your own green words are not hints about your partner's clues: only 3 of your 9 agents are theirs too.
      • Each of your assassins is an assassin for your partner one time in three, so be careful guessing them.
      • After a correct guess, a wrong guess that lands on a bystander costs nothing more than stopping. Only assassins are dangerous.
      • A clue that hit a bystander still points at its other words. Come back to them later.

      Why I built it

      TL;DR: I wanted a portfolio piece that incorporates an LLM so I built an AI partner for a fun co-op word game, ultimately using Google's open model Gemma 4 running on its own GPU.

      I love board games

      I've always loved board games. Cooperative board games like Codenames Duet are especially fun where you work towards a friend to solve a common goal. I enjoy adversarial games too, but teamwork is better for me than straining friendships.

      In 2021, I wanted to use some of the latest word analysis and AI tools to make an AI partner to play with. I worked on it, but it was a slog and ended up unfinished.

      Enter mid-2026 and Claude's Opus 5.5. Since that first attempt, genAI tech has come a long way and I wanted to try again. So I picked up my old repo and kept going. I built the first full working game in about 3 days, and have been tinkering since then.

      The problem

      Word association games should be a perfect problem for an LLM. But the game isn't just about creating word associations, it's about theory of mind: how is the other player going to read the clues you give?

      To solve this, every version played the same 8 boards with an outside player (Claude Sonnet, following its own guide), to tune the Basic model and find the best instructions for the Advanced model.

      How the partners evolved

      I started with a random baseline, before fleshing out the GloVe + Bayesian partner, then trialling different LLMs and eventually finding Google's Gemma to be the best small model performer. Sonnet would cost less per game than the GPU for a single player, but I get $30 of credits for free with Modal each month so I may as well use them. Running an open model myself was part of the learning too, rather than just plugging in an API key.

      Opus 5.5 at max effort played well but was slow (about 2 minutes a clue) and cost over $1 a game. Not the right fit for a personal site, so Advanced stays with Gemma.

      The architecture

      Lessons

      • I had figured a bigger model would be better, but gpt-oss-120b only tied the 31B Gemma (it did hit fewer assassins).
      • Letting the model rehearse as the guesser seemed to make its clues safer (see Inside one clue above).
      • I could experiment fast by setting some guardrails before letting Opus iterate on the reference player's guide. It got up to v10, but results had plateaued, so I kept v9.
      • Often I could get better performance by pointing Opus at something unsatisfying and telling it to improve it. It didn't seem to do much of this step by itself, and needed the prompting.
      • Given the time and cost, I didn't run thousands of experiments so I used smaller samples (8 boards) and checked the key comparisons with paired tests (a sign test on each board, plus a bootstrap interval).

      Making it faster

      • About half of Gemma's turns took over 30 seconds, which ended up being a weird quirk in how it wrote its JSON replies: one test reply would pad itself with blank space until it hit the token limit.
      • The fix was forcing compact JSON with no whitespace so it could no longer ramble, and testing its ideas went from a max of 36 seconds to ~6.
      • I tried other things to improve response speed like cutting from 8 clue ideas to 6. It saved about 2s, but the testing looked worse, so I kept 8.
      • I also added some better UX alongside it: the GPU starts waking the moment the Advanced button is pressed, the board shows straight away while the GPU wakes up so players can start absorbing the words, and live progress messages say what the AI is doing.

      LLM cost and safety safeguards

      • The GPU is private (no public URL), sleeps when idle and never runs more than one. Advanced switches off at $27 of the month's $30 of credit, so Basic (GloVe on the CPU) keeps the game playable.
      • Cost controls on the server: daily cap on GPU games, timeouts, GPU warm-up only when Advanced is picked, fallback to Basic if the model fails.
      • Rude-word and political filter on AI clues (one game I played it said KINKY 2. Not the worst but best avoided 😅).
